I Built a Wind Tunnel That Can Be Four Wind Tunnels

I Built a Wind Tunnel That Can Be Four Wind Tunnels

Ayoub Attou2026-11-17

The finished closed-circuit tunnel in the lab. Yes, it really is that blue.

There is a moment in every big engineering project when the thing stops being a drawing. For me it came when a 45 kW fan spun up in our lab and air began moving through a duct that, not long before, had existed only as lines in CAD software and arguments in my notebook.

This post is about the road to that moment, and the road after it. I want to show you the whole process rather than just the result: the early ideas I had to abandon, the calculations I got wrong or had to redo, the weeks of waiting on a factory, the day the measurements came back noisy, and the numbers that made me happy next to the ones that still make me frown. If you are thinking about building a piece of experimental equipment yourself, I hope the honest version is more useful than the polished one.

The problem: 50 m/s was not going to cut it

Every lab has something it wishes it could do but can’t. Ours was simple: test things in fast air. The quickest wind tunnel available at our university topped out at around 50 m/s. That is fine for a teaching demonstration, but it falls well short of what my research group actually wanted to do: aerodynamics of drones, car-style models at highway speeds, flow visualisation with advanced optical methods, and cold-flow crossflow flame experiments for combustion research.

Speed matters more than it might seem. In fluid mechanics, a flow’s behaviour depends on a dimensionless number, the Reynolds number, which compares inertial forces to viscous ones. A small model at low speed can have a very different Reynolds number from the real thing, and flows can behave differently as a result. You can’t always match it perfectly, but the faster your tunnel, the closer you can get to realistic conditions. A drone rotor tip, a car mirror and a wing section all behave more like their real-life selves when you can push air past them quickly.

Buying a commercial tunnel that does all of that was never realistic. Truly multi-mode facilities exist, but they belong to national laboratories, and they cost tens to hundreds of millions of RMB and took years to build. Every university tunnel I found in the literature committed to one operating mode and lived with it. A lab wanting to do smoke visualisation, high-speed measurement and combustion work would normally need three separate tunnels, and floor space we simply didn’t have.

So the plan became slightly ridiculous for a master’s project: design and build one tunnel that can reconfigure itself into several different tunnels, and make it fast. I wanted it to be a lasting tool for the department, not a demonstration piece that gets switched on twice and then collects dust.

THE TIMELINE AT A GLANCE

Sketching the dream (and cutting half of it)

The very first project sheet is almost comically ambitious. It listed four goals: aerodynamic tests on models, cold-flow crossflow flames, creating pulsating flow upstream of the test section, and testing drones. Next to the pulsating-flow goal I wrote, in brackets, “very hard”. That was an accurate prediction. My first speed target was a more modest 80 m/s, about 288 km/h, and I also wrote down a pulsation frequency of 200 Hz with an amplitude of 20 m/s.

The pulsation goal came with what I thought was my cleverest feature: a wall of small fans, each individually controllable, so I could produce structured, time-varying inflow. It was a lovely idea, the kind of thing that gets a room of researchers nodding, and it did not survive the design process. In my notes it is now struck through, along with the 200 Hz requirement. I’ll explain why when we get to the fan, but the short version is that being clever and being fast are two different engineering goals, and for this project I had to choose.

Alongside the sketching I was reading. My notes from this period are full of references: a Chinese textbook on experimental fluid mechanics (which I photographed page by page), papers on automotive wind-tunnel flow quality, a paper on reactive control of velocity fluctuations with a deformable surface and real-time PIV, and commercial examples of 80 m/s open-jet tunnels and blow-down tunnels to see what the professionals were doing. I presented one of the papers to the group on 27 September, and that is roughly the point where the project stopped being an idea and became a plan.

Early concept CADs: closed circuit, a cutaway, blow-down and open circuit. The same bones, rearranged.

A quick tour: what is actually inside a wind tunnel?

Before going further, it helps to understand what each part of a tunnel does, because the rest of the story is about getting each of them right. Follow the air around the loop and you meet these in order.

  • The fan supplies the energy. Everything else in the tunnel exists to turn its rough, swirling output into something a measurement can trust.

  • The settling chamber is a wide, calm section where the flow can slow down and start to even out.

  • The honeycomb is a block of small parallel channels. It straightens the flow, removing swirl and large sideways motions, rather like combing tangled hair.

  • The contraction squeezes the flow into the small test section. Squeezing accelerates the air and, helpfully, makes the speed variations across the flow proportionally smaller. A good contraction is the heart of a good tunnel.

  • The test section is where the experiment lives. It needs steady, uniform air and good access for instruments and cameras.

  • The diffuser slows the air down again gradually, recovering pressure and wasting less energy. If it widens too quickly, the flow separates from the walls and the whole tunnel suffers.

  • The corners in a closed loop need turning vanes, or the air tumbles around the bend and arrives at the next section in a mess.

Get any one of these wrong and the flaw travels downstream into your data. That is what makes tunnel design humbling: there is no single hero component, and a perfect contraction can’t rescue a bad honeycomb.

The idea that made it work: modularity

Wind tunnels come in a few classic flavours, and each is good at something different:

TypeHow it worksGreat for
Open circuit (Eiffel)Sucks in lab air, blows it through, exhausts it.Smoke and tracer work; simple and cheap to build.
Closed circuit (Göttingen)Air recirculates around a loop.High, steady speeds and good flow quality for precise measurement.
Blow-downFan blows into an open space.Combustion experiments, because flames and hot exhaust never enter the tunnel loop.

Normally you pick one. The alternatives are building several tunnels, which costs a fortune and eats floor space, or filling a single tunnel with valves and dampers to redirect the air, which is complicated and prone to leaks. Leaks are the enemy of a fast tunnel: at these pressures, any gap becomes a whistling hole that disturbs the flow.

My approach was to split the machine into a fixed core (fan, diffuser, honeycomb, contraction, test section) and swappable modules that bolt on at standardised interfaces. Change the module, change the tunnel. I thought it was neat enough to write up as a patent disclosure. The draft describes one fixed section, replaceable sections for open, closed and blow-down operation, and a common quick-release flange so each swap seals properly without having to recalibrate everything. It also points out that the design is easy to extend: a future module with a heater or a pulsation generator could attach through the same interface.

By the time the tunnel was built, the idea had grown from three configurations to four: closed circuit, open circuit, blow-down, and a “big chamber” mode with a large-section test volume for drones and bigger models. Switching between them means opening or closing bolted panels at set locations in the circuit, with nothing more than a spanner and no structural disassembly.

How the four operating modes are routed through the same duct structure.

Back-of-the-envelope, then reality

My first design notes read like a student doing sizing by rule of thumb, which is exactly what they were. The test section would be 200 × 200 mm, with a length of one to two widths, so 400 mm. The diffuser would use an area ratio of roughly 3:1, about 55 cm square at the exit, and 80 cm to 1 m long (four to five times the test-section width is a common guideline). The honeycomb would have 5 mm cells, ten diameters deep, so 50 mm. The whole open-circuit version would be about 3 m long.

I also ran a few quick numbers to see whether the plan made sense. The Reynolds number based on the honeycomb cell diameter at 80 m/s came out at around 27,000. That is a lot higher than the roughly 2,000–4,000 range where pipe flow tips from laminar to turbulent, so the flow in each cell would be turbulent. The pressure drop across the honeycomb, assuming a loss coefficient of 0.15, came out around 588 Pa. That figure sounds tiny but is a useful reminder that every screen and honeycomb takes a bite out of the pressure your fan has to supply.

I also decided the first build should be the open circuit, since it is the simplest, and leave the interface ready for the closed loop. Then the real design process caught up with those early sketches, and almost everything got bigger.

Why one big fan beat many small ones

The fan was one of the most consequential early decisions, and it was where my fan-array idea finally lost. The literature has low-budget university tunnels built from arrays of small fans, and the appeal is obvious: individual control, modest cost, easy to buy. But the pressure rise needed for speeds above 120 m/s is demanding, and at that point arrays of small fans can’t compete on efficiency or reliability. One machine is also far easier to maintain than dozens. So I chose a 4-72-10C industrial centrifugal fan rated at 45 kW.

I won’t pretend this was painless. Giving up the controllable fan wall meant giving up the original pulsating-flow goal, at least for now. But a tunnel that can reach 125 m/s today is worth more than one that might have produced fancy fluctuations at a lower speed.

The 4-72-10C 45 kW centrifugal fan and its data sheet.

The contraction cone: the hardest part to design

The contraction takes the flow from the 1 m × 1 m settling chamber down to the 20 × 20 cm test section. That is a 25:1 area ratio over just 1 m of length. A large ratio is attractive because it suppresses velocity variations strongly. But a short, aggressive contraction risks the boundary layer separating from the walls, and then your fast tunnel becomes a fast mess.

I generated the wall profile in Python using a 5th-order polynomial, chosen so that the velocity gradient and curvature are zero at both ends of the contraction. In plain language, the walls start and finish tangent to the straight sections, with no abrupt change in slope, so the flow isn’t jolted on the way in or out. I plotted the curve, checked it, then exported the coordinates for CAD and for the people who would build it.

The Python used to generate the profile, and the resulting curve.

A honeycomb, but a much deeper one

Remember my 50 mm honeycomb? As I read more, I kept running into the same guideline: the length-to-diameter ratio of the honeycomb cells needs to be at least 80 to 100 to remove swirl effectively. My early ten-diameter rule of thumb was nowhere near that. With such an aggressive contraction behind it, I wanted the flow to arrive well conditioned, so I made the honeycomb generous.

The final honeycomb is aluminium, about 6 mm cells and 1,120 mm deep, giving an L/D of around 186, nearly double the recommended minimum. It spans the full 1 m × 1 m duct. It’s a big, heavy, slightly absurd block of metal, and it was deliberate: with only a metre of contraction, I couldn’t afford to give the flow any excuse to arrive badly behaved.

Technical drawing and CAD of the honeycomb assembly.

A test section you can see through

The test section stayed at 20 × 20 × 40 cm. That is big enough for small drone rotors, wing sections with reasonable chord and car parts like mirrors, and small enough that the model doesn’t block too much of the flow, even at top speed. The walls are transparent acrylic on all four sides, so cameras and lasers can see in without any structural compromise. That choice mattered a lot for my group, whose research leans heavily on optical measurement.

The transparent acrylic test section, and the big-chamber configuration.

Diffuser and corners

Downstream of the test section, the flow passes through a diffuser expanding at a 10° half-angle. That is conservative enough to keep the boundary layer attached along the walls while recovering a useful amount of static pressure before the next corner. At each of the four 90° bends in the closed-circuit loop, welded steel turning vanes redirect the flow with minimal total pressure loss. Neither of these gets any glory, but a closed-circuit tunnel with poor corners and a poor diffuser will lose most of its power to them.

Into the factory

Fabrication ran from October 2024 to April 2025 at a metalworking factory about 2 km from campus. That turned out to be a real advantage. I could walk over, look at the parts in progress and bring finished pieces back to the lab easily. In February I prepared the full set of 2D drawings for every part: corners, honeycomb frame, contraction, diffuser, extensions, test section, and made one of my site visits on 7 June. Final assembly, all electrical work and the testing were done on-site in the lab.

The settling chamber, straight ducts, diffuser and corners were cut and TIG-welded from 3–5 mm mild-steel plate, all at the same 1 m × 1 m cross-section. That uniform cross-section was the decision that made everything else possible: standard plate throughout, manageable cost, and interchangeable mode-switching panels. Inside, the steel got a matt finish, partly to cut reflections for optical flow visualisation and partly to protect it from moisture in the lab air.

Welding the 1 m × 1 m duct sections, and the finished settling chamber and diffuser.

The contraction: weeks of sanding

If you ask which part was the most work, it is the contraction cone. The factory first built a steel skeleton with transverse formers every 100 mm, each cut to the computed polynomial coordinates. Then the outer skin went over the skeleton, followed by multiple layers of filler, with extensive sanding between coats.

Why so much effort on a surface? Because at these speeds, roughness can trigger the boundary layer to become turbulent early, and a thick turbulent boundary layer is exactly what grows into the non-uniformity you are trying to avoid. The finished interior is mirror-smooth, and here, quite literally, surface finish is part of the aerodynamic design.

Dimensioned drawing of the contraction, and its steel skeleton during fabrication.

Small things that matter later

A few details came from thinking ahead to daily use. The honeycomb sits in a bolted steel frame so it can be slid out for inspection or cleaning without taking the ducting apart. That is an easy thing to overlook at the design stage and a very welcome one once the tunnel is in regular use. The fan is driven through a variable-frequency drive, controlled from an analogue panel over a 0–50 Hz range, with motor overload protection. Three emergency-stop buttons are spread around the tunnel: at the control panel, next to the test section and at the laboratory entrance. A 45 kW motor deserves that much respect.

Assembly and the first spin

Once the pieces arrived at the lab, assembly proceeded from the fan outward: fan, settling chamber, honeycomb, contraction, test section, diffuser, return ducts. Every joint was sealed before the next section went on, and every flange was checked for air-tightness before the first power-on. My July notes list the jobs in blunt shorthand: install the narrow side first, commission the centrifugal fan, and fit the sensors, one hot-wire probe and six pressure probes.

The tunnel during final assembly.

Getting a 45 kW centrifugal fan to spin up and hold a set speed is a satisfying moment. It is also the moment when the project changes character. Until then, every problem is a design problem, which you can solve with a pencil. After that, they become measurement problems, which you can only solve by understanding what your instruments are telling you. And that was where the next stretch of the project went.

The part nobody warns you about: noise

When you want to know the pressure fluctuations in a fast flow, you need clean signals. Mine were not clean. In the January experiments the pressure traces were swamped with electrical noise, and the cause wasn’t aerodynamics at all but wiring. A 45 kW motor and its drive are about as electrically noisy as a lab gets, and small pressure-sensor signals are easy prey.

The first fix I tried worked: joining the negative of the power supply to the negative of the signal line, so the two shared a common reference. The noise disappeared. The photos from that session show the improvised nature of the work, with sensor leads running across the tunnel’s steel frame and what looks like foil wrapped around the connections.

Sensor wiring on the tunnel frame during the noise-hunting session.

With cleaner data, the next step was frequency analysis. My notes say the fluctuations of interest are around 100 Hz, and that I wanted records about a second long. So I logged pressure time-series at each fan setting and ran Fast Fourier Transforms in Python with NumPy, an FFT being a way of splitting a signal into the frequencies it contains. I compared three sensor positions across all fan frequencies, looking for fan blade-pass tones, structural resonances or any other periodic content that would reveal unsteadiness in the flow.

FFT spectra of the pressure data, compared across sensor positions and fan frequencies.

I’m still working through exactly what those spectra say, so I won’t pretend I’ve got a tidy conclusion yet. Peaks that scale with fan speed would point to the fan itself, while peaks that stay put would suggest something structural. I’ll report what I find once I’ve matched the spectra against the fan’s blade count and speed. That is part of the honest picture: some measurements raise more questions than they answer, at least at first.

Finally, the numbers

How I measured

Velocity measurements used a Pitot-static tube connected to a calibrated digital manometer with 0.1 Pa resolution. A Pitot tube measures the difference between total and static pressure, and that difference, the dynamic pressure, gives you the speed. I moved the probe by hand using a lead-screw positioner. I started with a 9-point check and then moved to a full 11 × 11 grid, 121 measurement points per run, at five fan frequencies from 10 to 50 Hz. Room temperature during the campaign was between 20 and 24 °C.

The Pitot-static tube traversing the test section by hand.

My notebook from the early runs is exactly what you would expect: hand-drawn cubes showing where I stood the probe, measurement positions in five 10 cm steps, vertical and horizontal sweeps, and some entries that just say “max”, meaning the reading had gone off the instrument’s range. Not pretty, but it is the real thing.

Lab notebook from the early speed runs.

Speed: did we hit the target?

Yes. At 50 Hz, the closed-circuit tunnel reached a peak of 127.9 m/s and a central-core average of 124.8 m/s. The design target was 125 m/s, so in the central test region we got there. The full-plane mean is a little lower at 121.1 m/s, which is expected, since the slower air near the walls pulls the average down.

Mean velocity vs. fan frequency in the different modes. Closed circuit is almost perfectly linear.

The relationship between drive frequency and speed is beautifully linear: about 2.5 m/s for every hertz, with an R² of 0.9995 (2.53 m/s per Hz for the core, 2.45 for the full-plane mean). That’s what physics predicts. Fan speed is proportional to drive frequency, and the volume of air the fan moves is proportional to fan speed, so the speed in the test section should follow. A tunnel whose speed you can predict from a dial setting is a tunnel you can actually run experiments in, and it is also a quiet sign that the system is behaving sensibly and that nothing is choking or leaking badly.

For context, at 50 Hz the core flow is about Mach 0.36, with a dynamic pressure near 9.4 kPa and a Reynolds number of about 1.7 million based on the 0.2 m section width. That is a serious flow for a laboratory this size, and it is the regime in which the drones, vehicle components and wing sections we care about start behaving realistically.

Is the flow any good?

Speed alone doesn’t make a good tunnel. A fast tunnel with lumpy flow produces fast, lumpy data. For subsonic tunnels, a commonly cited benchmark for professional-grade flow is velocity non-uniformity below ±3 %. I measured it in two ways. The first is the root-mean-square non-uniformity: the standard deviation of the 121 point velocities divided by their mean, which is the measure behind the ±3 % benchmark. The second is the half-range, (Vmax − Vmin) divided by twice the mean, a stricter measure because a single outlier can dominate it.

By the rms measure, the results were encouraging: 2.5 to 2.8 % over the whole plane at every speed, and 1.4 to 2.4 % in the central 5 × 5 region. By the stricter half-range measure, the numbers are less flattering: about ±5.4 to 6.6 % over the full plane, within ±3 % only at 50 Hz. In other words, the bulk of the flow is uniform, but there are a few points, mostly near the walls, that sit noticeably lower. An earlier 9-point check gave a similar story, with core uniformity between about ±2.1 and ±2.8 % across 26 to 126 m/s and boundary layers of roughly 30 to 40 mm on each wall.

Velocity maps: closed circuit at 50 Hz (left) and big chamber at 30 Hz (right).

What stands out in the maps is a consistent pattern: the centre of the test section runs about 4 to 6 % faster than the outer ring of points, and that ratio barely changes from 10 to 50 Hz. This matters for the interpretation. If the non-uniformity came from fan unsteadiness or random turbulence, it would change as I turned the speed up. A pattern that stays the same shape at every speed points instead to geometry: the wall boundary layers and the way the flow leaves the contraction. At 50 Hz, the row-mean velocity peaks around 124 m/s near the centre and drops to about 118 m/s at the top wall and 117 m/s at the bottom, a slight asymmetry of about 1 % that I’ll want to investigate. Some of the edge deficit may also come from my probe disturbing the flow close to the acrylic walls, so it may be a little exaggerated.

Given that this is a 25:1 contraction in only 1 m of length, I was happy. A short, aggressive contraction is exactly the kind that tends to cause trouble, and the result suggests the polynomial profile, the deep honeycomb and the hours of sanding did their jobs. There is no single dominant factor here; the result looks like the compound effect of several things done reasonably well at the same time.

How much should you trust these numbers?

It’s worth being straightforward about uncertainty. The instrument itself is not the limiting factor: 0.1 Pa corresponds to a velocity resolution of about 0.02 m/s at 10 Hz and well under 0.001 m/s at 50 Hz. The real contributors are probe positioning by hand, unsteadiness in the flow at each grid point (point-to-point scatter of roughly 0.7 m/s at 10 Hz rising to 3.4 m/s at 50 Hz), and, above about 40 Hz, compressibility. Beyond Mach 0.3, the simple incompressible Pitot formula over-reads the velocity by about M²/8, roughly 1.7 % at 125 m/s. I also haven’t yet repeated full traverses, so I can’t quote run-to-run repeatability. That is the first thing on my to-do list, and until it’s done, I treat the third significant figure of any number in this post with caution.

The things that didn’t add up

A blog that only shows the green checkmarks would be lying to you. Here is what I’m still chewing on.

  • The big chamber misbehaves at 30 Hz. At 20 and 40 Hz, it is uniform, but at 30 Hz the profile turns jet-like: the centre runs at about 17.6 m/s against 12.5 m/s at the edges, a non-uniformity of 17 %. My suspects are leakage at the access-panel joints or flow separating upstream of the chamber. Until I’ve chased it down, I wouldn’t trust that operating point for quantitative tests, and that is exactly why I’m glad I measured every frequency instead of just the ones I expected to look good.

  • The fan runs out of breath in the big chamber. The mean velocity grows by roughly 0.7 m/s per Hz between 20 and 40 Hz, then only about 0.2 m/s per Hz between 40 and 50 Hz. In a linear system, that flattening usually means the fan is approaching its flow limit with this configuration’s resistance. It is a useful reminder that a tunnel’s top speed depends on the whole circuit, not just the fan on its data sheet.

  • Open-circuit and blow-down results look almost too good. Their profiles come out nearly flat, with a core-to-edge ratio of about 1.00, even though they use the same contraction and test section as the closed circuit, which showed a clear 4–6 % edge deficit. If the geometry causes the deficit in one mode, I’d expect to see it in all of them. On top of that, the two datasets differ from each other by a near-constant offset with almost no scatter, far less than the real-flow scatter I saw in the closed-circuit grids. I take that as a sign something in my measurement or processing needs rechecking, so I’m calling both datasets preliminary until repeat traverses confirm or overturn them.

  • Temperature. After 15-minute runs, the closed circuit warms up slightly due to friction. That is normal, since the energy the fan puts in all eventually becomes heat, but it is another reason to log temperature during long experiments.

The first real customer: a drone in a crosswind

A tunnel isn’t finished until it has done a real job. The first one was a drone stability test in big-chamber mode. The question was simple: can a commercial multirotor’s autopilot hold a hover under a steady crosswind, and can we measure how it responds?

Making it safe

Safety came first. A fine-mesh net across the upstream face of the chamber kept the drone away from the fan inlet if it lost control, and a lightweight tether anchored above the chamber provided a physical backstop. I kept the tether long and slack on purpose, so that it wouldn’t interfere with the drone’s own dynamics. I set the wind to 8 m/s, just above 20 Hz on the drive, which is around 29 km/h. The manufacturer’s limit is 10 m/s, so 8 m/s is a real challenge for the drone while still inside its flight envelope. It is also in the range where the big-chamber velocity grid is uniform, which is another reason to choose that frequency.

Measuring where the drone is

The easy option would have been to read the drone’s own GPS and inertial data. But a fan motor’s electromagnetic interference can corrupt those, and they drift over time. So I built an independent tracking system. Two cameras outside the acrylic walls gave a side view and a top view, and a custom Python program found the drone’s centre in each frame using colour thresholding and converted pixels to millimetres.

For calibration, I printed a checkerboard across ten A4 sheets, assembled them into one big board mounted on an acrylic backing, and photographed it in many positions and orientations. Standard OpenCV routines then extracted each camera’s matrix and lens distortion. The result was about 1.12 mm per pixel for the side camera and 0.60 mm per pixel for the top camera, which in principle gives position resolution better than ±1 mm at the drone’s hover point.

The drone in the big chamber, the checkerboard calibration target, and the live tracking views.

What happened

With the safety net, tether and tracking all active, I commanded the drone to hover and brought the fan up gradually to 8 m/s. When the wind reached the drone, it drifted sideways. By the side-camera data, the horizontal coordinate moved from about 1,035 mm to 774 mm, a drift of roughly 261 mm, while the vertical coordinate changed by only 3.4 mm. That is almost pure lateral displacement, exactly what a crosswind should do.

Then the interesting part: the drone’s flight controller sensed the position error and fought back, increasing thrust on the upwind rotors and reducing it on the downwind ones. It recovered toward its set-point and then oscillated around it, with the oscillation amplitude staying bounded throughout. The tether never went taut, so the drone was genuinely flying itself rather than hanging on the string.

What I’d fix next time

This was a first exploratory run, and I know what is wrong with it. The tether was a little too short and stiff and sometimes constrained the drone’s yaw. I had no anemometer inside the chamber during the run, so the wind speed was inferred from earlier calibration rather than measured live. Leakage through the access-panel joints affects the flow at the drone’s location. And I only extracted a few position samples from the log, which show the drift but not the recovery. Maximum excursion, RMS oscillation and recovery time are still to be computed from the full tracking record. Looking further ahead, combining the two cameras into a synchronised stereo system would give full 3D position tracking.

Even so, the main result stands: the big-chamber mode works as a test environment for full-size drones, and a pair of cameras and a printed checkerboard is a low-cost way to measure position inside a tunnel. For a first customer, I’ll take that.

So what did this actually achieve?

Step back from the numbers for a moment. Most student-built tunnels in the literature operate somewhere between 40 and 80 m/s, usually with a single dedicated fan arrangement and a test section sized for convenience. Reaching about 125 m/s with one off-the-shelf industrial fan, while keeping flow uniformity inside a professional benchmark, required getting several things right at once: the contraction profile, the honeycomb sizing, the surface finish inside the contraction and the diffuser geometry.

Three things from the project feel worth keeping. First, standard 1 m × 1 m industrial ducting, finished carefully and paired with a purpose-designed contraction, can deliver performance comparable to bespoke research hardware. Second, for speeds above 120 m/s, a single high-power centrifugal fan was the right call over an array of smaller ones, for efficiency and for maintenance. Third, the multi-mode platform now exists, and it can evolve with whatever the next student or professor wants to try. Undergraduate project groups have been using the tunnel since commissioning, which was always the intention: a shared facility that people can build on instead of starting from zero.

What I’d tell someone starting this

If you take only a few things from this post, take these.

  • Pick the one decision that unlocks everything else. For me it was the uniform 1 m × 1 m cross-section. It simplified fabrication, cost and mode switching all at once.

  • Treat your first numbers as placeholders. My early honeycomb was 50 mm deep and the final one is 1,120 mm. Learn from the literature early, and expect your sizing to grow.

  • Let go of clever ideas that fight the physics. The controllable fan wall was elegant. The single big centrifugal fan was right.

  • Surface finish is aerodynamics. The hours of sanding on the contraction are part of the design, not a cosmetic extra.

  • Choose a fabricator you can walk to. Being 2 km from the factory saved enormous amounts of time and let me catch problems early.

  • Expect the instrumentation to cause more trouble than the tunnel. Grounding, noise and calibration ate real time, and they are easy to underestimate.

  • Measure everything, and report what doesn’t fit. The anomalies at 30 Hz and in the preliminary modes are the best to-do list I could ask for.

What’s next

Several things are lined up. First, repeat traverses to nail down uniformity and repeatability, including an investigation of the big-chamber anomaly. Second, a proper in-situ anemometer and longer, lighter tether for the drone tests, followed by a full analysis of the recovery dynamics. Third, the FFT work, which needs to be matched against the fan’s blade-pass frequency before I say anything firm. My project log also lists the jet diffusion flame-length experiments, the cold-flow and combustion work that started this whole story, for April 2026. And I’m writing the tunnel up as a paper, aimed at a journal such as Flow Measurement and Instrumentation.

It’s a strange feeling to finish something that began as a sentence on a project form: build a small low-speed wind tunnel. The finished machine is much bigger, much faster and a good deal bluer than that sentence suggested. What I’m most pleased with is not the 127.9 m/s, but that the next person who walks into our lab with a question about fast air will have somewhere to ask it.
